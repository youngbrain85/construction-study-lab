1The problem

Put your calculator on the desk. This is one complete mix design, from the problem statement to a checked batch, in the same ten steps as Part 1. Nothing is skipped, and nothing comes from software.

Read it carefully before touching the calculator. Two things matter more than the rest. Well-rounded gravel: that is the 8 percent water reduction from Part 1. And exposure F0/S0/W0/C0: indoor and protected, so durability will not control the w/cm — strength will.

2Steps 1–3 — Slump, size, water and air

Method: Part 1, steps 1–3

Steps 1 and 2 — given

The slump is given: 3–4 in. The NMAS is given: 1 in. In a homework problem these may not be given, and then you choose them yourself with the rules in Part 1.

Step 3 — water and air from the table

Enter Table 2 with a 3–4 in. slump and a 1 in. NMAS, in the non-air-entrained block — this is indoor concrete, so no entrained air is needed:

  • Initial water estimate: 325 lb/yd³
  • Entrapped air: about 1.5%, which is 0.015 yd³ of our cubic yard. We need that volume in step 7.

Step 3, continued — adjust for particle shape

The gravel is well rounded, so start with an 8 percent reduction:

325 × 0.92 ≈ 299 lb/yd³
Write it down

Design water: 299 lb/yd³. Everything from here on depends on it.

3Step 4 — Required strength and the w/cm ratio

Method: Part 1, step 4

Required average strength

The specified strength is 3,000 psi, and the plant has no acceptable strength record, so we use the ACI margin for the 3,000 to 5,000 psi range:

f′cr = 3,000 + 1,200 = 4,200 psi

Notice: we design for 4,200 psi, but the drawing still says 3,000. The extra strength is the safety margin against batch-to-batch variation.

Water–cementitious materials ratio

From Table 5, non-air-entrained: 4,000 psi → 0.57 and 5,000 psi → 0.48. Our 4,200 psi sits between them, 200 psi into a 1,000 psi interval — 20 percent of the way:

w/cm = 0.57 − 0.20 × (0.57 − 0.48) = 0.57 − 0.018 ≈ 0.55

Now check durability. Exposure F0/S0/W0/C0 sets no maximum w/cm, so there is nothing lower to compare against. 0.55 governs. Strength controls this design.

4Step 5 — Total cementitious materials

Method: Part 1, step 5

Water divided by the ratio:

CM = 299 lb/yd³0.55 ≈ 544 lb/yd³
Write it down

Cementitious materials: 544 lb/yd³. We need it for the volume calculation in step 7.

5Step 6 — Coarse aggregate

Method: Part 1, step 6

Enter Table 6 with a 1 in. NMAS and a sand fineness modulus of 2.60. Read across, read down: b/b0 = 0.69.

bulk volume = 0.69 × 27 ft³/yd³ ≈ 18.6 ft³
mCA, OD = 0.69 × 27 × 100 lb/ft³ = 1,863 lb/yd³

The volume formula in the next step wants the SSD mass, not oven-dry. Absorption is 0.5 percent, so:

mCA, SSD = 1,863 × 1.005 ≈ 1,872 lb/yd³
Watch out

b/b0 uses the dry-rodded bulk density, which includes the voids between the stones. That is fine — the sand and the paste fill those voids in step 7 — but it is why you must not treat this bulk volume as an absolute volume.

6Step 7 — Fine aggregate

Method: Part 1, step 7

Let's fill the box. Volume equals mass divided by relative density times 1,685 lb/yd³:

Absolute volumes of everything except the sand
IngredientCalculationVolume, yd³
Cementitious materials544 ÷ (3.15 × 1,685)0.102
Water299 ÷ (1.00 × 1,685)0.177
Coarse aggregate (SSD)1,872 ÷ (2.68 × 1,685)0.415
Air1.5% × 1.0 yd³0.015
Sum0.7095

Water has no relative density term because water is the reference: its relative density is 1. The sand takes what is left:

VFA = 1 − 0.7095 = 0.2905 yd³
mFA, SSD = 0.2905 × 2.64 × 1,685 ≈ 1,292 lb/yd³

7Step 8 — The design-basis mix

Method: Part 1, steps 8–10

Three quick checks:

  1. The ratio. 299 ÷ 544 ≈ 0.55 — matches the design.
  2. The volume. 0.102 + 0.177 + 0.415 + 0.015 + 0.2905 = 1.000 yd³.
  3. The density. 4,007 lb ÷ 27 ft³ ≈ 148.4 lb/ft³. Normal-weight concrete runs about 140 to 150 lb/ft³, so the answer is reasonable.

8Step 9 — Moisture adjustment

Method: Part 1, step 9

Say the coarse-aggregate stockpile is wet: total moisture content MC = 2.0 percent, against an absorption A = 0.5 percent. The free moisture is the difference:

free moisture = 2.0% − 0.5% = 1.5%

On 1,872 lb SSD (about 1,863 lb oven-dry), that is roughly 28 lb of free water riding in on the stones. Two corrections follow:

batch coarse aggregate = 1,872 × 1 + 0.0201 + 0.005 ≈ 1,900 lb
batch water = 299 − 28 = 271 lb (coarse aggregate only)

Then repeat the same two lines for the sand with its own moisture content and its 0.7 percent absorption, and subtract its free water too. Try it: if the sand is at MC = 5.0 percent, how much water is left to add at the mixer?

Answer: about 55 lb of free water from the sand, so about 216 lb of batch water and about 1,347 lb of wet sand.

9Step 10 — Trial batch

Method: Part 1, step 10

Batch the corrected weights and measure what the paper assumed:

  • Slump (ASTM C143) — is it 3–4 in.? If not, adjust the water or the water-reducer dose, not the w/cm.
  • Air content — near 1.5 percent for this non-air-entrained mix.
  • Unit weight — close to the theoretical 148 lb/ft³. A big gap means the volumes or the moisture corrections are off.
  • Strength — cylinders at 28 days (ASTM C39), expecting an average near 4,200 psi.

Refine, batch again, and only then call the mix done. Paper is a starting point, not the final mix.
